---
title: Immortality
author: Matthew
layout: post
---

There is this concept of humans as mosaics that I have always been particularly fond of. Much like how mosaics are works of art composed of small or amorphous objects like colored stones, glass, or ceramics our identities are assembled similarly by the people and moments that have touched our lives. The key idea being that our existence is patchwork built on quiet exchanges where it is this constant, implicit giving and receiving that threads us together

We are mosaics of everyone who we have ever encountered, whether the connection was deep, transient, questionable, or painful, and they linger within us long after they have departed. For some you can see it in the music they listen to, their style, their humor, the habits they carry, or the kindness they show, among many others. I see it in myself all the time. I fell in love with writing cards because my grandfather used to send me letters with newspapers clippings just because they reminded him of me. My kindness towards service workers first stemmed from an admiration of my mother who seemingly knew everyone everywhere whenever we would do our weekend chores. I reuse my father’s jokes because they remind me of him, and when people laugh they are laughing because of him. I insist of paying at the restaurant because in my family the battle for the bill was always an expression of love. I pick up fallen scooters and bikes because I noticed my friend would every time we would walk together. My closet, filled with gifts and thrifted treasurers, is an ode to others – a living archive of the people I have known. Many of the shortcuts I take, the nooks where I pause, and the quiet cafes I breathe in were shared with my closest friends. Even the movies, music, books, and art that I love can be traced back to someone who cared enough to shared them. When I look at myself and when I look how I move in the world, I am constantly reminded of the people who shaped me to be this way. As the famous tumblr user viridianmasquerade put it “I am a mosaic of everyone I’ve ever loved, even for a heartbeat.”

At first this realization can feel a bit humbling, especially in a culture that prizes hyper individuality and exceptionalism, because it shatters the illusion that we are self-contained, wholly original beings. As collections of every soul who has cared for you the perception that we are special or abstruse seemingly evaporates. Yet, I want to argue that this can be both empowering and moving, not diminishing. Because just as others live on through us, so too do we live on through others. The marks we leave, the kindness we show, the ideas we share, the habits we inspire, become pieces of someone else’s mosaic

I’ve never been one to be obsessed with the fantasy of a corporeal immortality (or eternal youth), my heart is too fragile to bear the consequences of a life that simply never ends. To watch the world around me crumble, dissolve, and rebuild itself endlessly could never be worth a life with limitless hedonism, shoutout Dorian Gray I guess. But I have come to believe in another form of immortality: the immortality of influence. Not in the legacy of names etched into history books or hung up in the rafters, but one embedded in everyday lives, carried forward through actions, ideas, and compassion, and built on the foundation of the human mosaic.

Once you truly recognize that we are shaped by countless others can you begin to see that people already live forever. My mother lives in me through the lessons passed down to me by her mother, shared by her grandmother, and taught by her great grandmother, so on and so forth. I will live in my sister, her future family, and the people that she loves, through the tenderness and affection we’ve shared with each other. My friends and peers live in me through their words and habits which ripple outward to all those that I meet. This transference of influence, a kind of transitive property of immortality, forms a kind of spiritual continuity that transcends death.

I find comfort in knowing that my life continues both in small, unseen ways but also in the lives I affect. Maybe I am in the kindness someone extends because of something I once said, in the creativity sparked by conversation (or even my essays???), in the words they repeat, in the ways that they love, in the laughter shared over old stories or recycled jokes, or even in the futures they imagine or create for themselves. Sure, I might be just a single voice in a chorus of influence, for some a larger and louder voice than others, but I am still there. You appreciate then how this idea exists diametrically to the concept of physical death as finality. Even thinking to the quote that posits “you die twice, once when you stop breathing and a second time, a bit later on, when somebody says you name for the last time,” fails to realize that life doesn’t have to end with your name or last memory. Because if we live on through the ways we’ve shaped others, then perhaps we never really die at all.

This way of thinking has always brought me comfort because I believe the energy of others travel and live on in multifaceted ways. Friends and family physically leave us and sometimes they return, but more often than not they become casualties to the slow drift of time. Yet I know I can feel them again at the park we used to gossip or the table we shared our first cup of coffee. Even after leaving the places I’ve called home I know they present in the tea we drank together every Saturday, the TV shows we used to gush over, or in the flowers that remind me of them. And yes, even in the mistakes. Nobody is ever able to truly actualize the most “ideal” version of themselves, but each time I pause before repeating a mistake that once caused pain, I like to imagine the spirits of those who I failed before guiding me toward something better. Former partners, old friends, and those who have passed continue to live through the quiet practice of improvement. Perhaps it is naïve, but I find solace in the belief that I can still give life to those who I’ve harmed or failed by striving to be better because of them.

My mom always used to tell me that when I look at our home that I shouldn’t see it as just a house. Instead, she asked me to view it as an accumulation of sacrifice, labor, and love made by generations of our family and friends that have allowed us to live safely and comfortably. Many of those before me I will never know, but as you have grown accustomed to hear, they continue to live within me. Within the opportunities I have, in the kindness I show, the places I live, and in the words that I write. Their lives echo through my own.

We often think of the butterfly effect in terms of tragedy and deleterious consequence, but what if we viewed it through the lens of the human mosaic. What if our small acts of care and creativity serve as the wingbeats that domino into a better world. I am, and always will be, a mosaic made up of the best parts of those I have loved. Even those whose lives intersected with mine briefly. My body may not be immortal, but my spirit carried in others already is.

Robin Williams once shared that “I don't know how much value I have in this universe, but I do know that I've made a few people happier than they would have been without me, and as long as I know that, I'm as rich as I ever need to be.” That, to me, captures the essence of the human mosaic as it relates to immortality. To find meaning not in permanence or achievement, but in connection; not in self-preservation, but in the joy of having made other people’s lives better and brighter. To know that you live on in a spirit of impact that spurs on more happiness, more creation, and more improvement. That kind of immortality is enough for me.
