There is a semi-popular tumblr post that sneaks its way back onto my timeline whenever I go on my more morose liking sprees. It asks why we allow our more positive, more affirming perceptions of ourselves to become delusion, while our more critical emotions are realistic. And while I agree with the OP for the most part, at least where it matters, I’ve got some experiences that give credence to the point it is pushing back against.


<span class="image right"><img src="{{ 'assets/images/blogpost/firstblogIG/larosa.jpeg' | relative_url }}" alt="" /></span>I don’t know about everyone else, but for me whenever I experience happiness in many ways I really am transported. Swept away by the current of joy into what feels like another world, a thoughtless focus where continued delight is my one - my only priority. It’s often why I tend to overstay my welcome or let time slip away regardless of tomorrow’s consequence. If you ever hang out with me, it’s unlikely I ever initiate the end of our time together. For the most part. One of my earliest memories is learning the old adage that time just moves faster when you are having fun and for me being happy is another world I wish I never had to leave. So why not stay, let yourself continue to experience happiness. At least I do, responsibility or reality will always be there to meet you after. 


On the other hand sadness and all its many forms are always a sharp reminder of my reality. My limitations, my faults, my obstacles… all the factors outside of my control. It’s not to say that sadness and all things negative are reality or my reality for that matter, rather it feels as if reality, logic, and grief are somewhat intertwined. Sides of the same coin. While happiness is limitless in its potential, its grandeur, its scope. Maybe I’m marred by joy's inexplicable brevity, but it is different to me. I know this to be true.


I bring this up because I have a habit of using reality as a crutch for grief. That’s “just the way things go, the way it is, what happens.” I tell myself these things to keep it pushing, that’s life. But I've struggled to embrace this mentality when I “mourn” over a life that is not my reality. How do you mourn something you never truly had. 


<span class="image left"><img src="{{ 'assets/images/blogpost/firstblogIG/Snowheart.jpeg' | relative_url }}" alt="" /></span>Bear with me here, but over the last few years I have become abundantly more and more aware of how death has steadily outgrown concept and become reality. In youth’s ignorance death and its consequences have not necessarily been a feeling I have had to reckon with. Relegated to a valiant if not emotional character death or in whispers that permeate the occasional family event (among other things). It’s not uncommon for adults to try and protect the youth from death's emotional grip.


Today, as death slowly ferries more and more around me to their new otherworldly beginnings I have found some ways to keep living in a world without those who have touched me in one way way or another. Despite it’s inexplicable weirdness. At the end of the day there are people you prepare to lose, there are also many who you don’t. Recently though, I’ve been wondering how to mourn those I never had a chance to get close with.


For those who don’t know I’ve always been far from my family. I don’t mean just distant in a metaphorical sense, I mean I have always literally been far from family. Growing up I was always so jealous of my friends who were close with their grandparents. Don’t get me wrong I love my grandparents and I couldn’t be here without them, their sacrifice, their hard work, or their love. I still manage to hold deep emotions for them even though I know so little about them. But they passed when I was young and when our times intersected they lived an ocean and a continent away.


<p><span class="image left"><img src="{{ 'assets/images/blogpost/firstblogIG/nightwalking.jpeg' | relative_url }}" alt="" /></span>I was thinking about this the other day but I don’t think my grandparents ever really had the chance to see me cry - I mean really cry, not the childlike tears you shed after some trivial matter you don’t quite fully understand. I don’t think my grandparents ever saw me sad, ever had the chance to comfort me, ever lift my spirits even when they didn’t know I was hurting. And it’s not just them, I don’t even know what their favorite color was, if they liked writing letters, what their dreams were when they were young, and many unfilled gaps in between. So when my grandpa passed after a long battle, a few years ago right smack dab in the middle of a “post” pandemic life I was so exceedingly lost. 

 
  I remember it kind of clearly, my mom texted me in the middle of a sunny SoCal afternoon, or was it around lunch time. I remember just walking back to my dorm after class and running into my friend, I told them and they asked me if I would join them shopping. Expecting to be overridden with grief, akin to how many of my friends and family had reacted in the past, I was struck by how unaffected I seemed. I spiraled for a while, wondering why I was acting this way. I grew more distraught over the fact that I hadn’t had the reaction I was expecting. I began grieving over my lack of grief.

 
  Mourning can be fickle. I never got an answer to my question if I’m being honest. Even today I don’t know how to fully feel, years after both of them passed on. I don’t have the solace of a relationship with impact, to be burdened by the hole left by the impact of someone with that much meaning. Nor do I have the excuse, the veil, of bitterness and anger. Instead I have fragments of a broken mirror that can never fully be pieced together. An ice cream shared on a humid summer day, the Skype calls with the exact same questions every time, the stories I hear whenever I visit where they used to live. Their old house.


  I miss them. Even if I don’t fully remember them.


  <span class="image left"><img src="{{ 'assets/images/blogpost/firstblogIG/sunset.jpeg' | relative_url }}" alt="" /></span>I don’t think there ever will be an answer. It also seems like others don’t have answers, losing someone and the feelings associated with it can’t just be replaced or filled with some matter of other things. Perhaps on some level growing up takes learning how to be comfortable with grief. Not like mourning comes with an instruction manual, let alone for something as incoherent and abstract as a relationship you yearn for. I guess, for now, I will do my best to channel it forward. I may not have known them, but I can get to know their legacy. I can strive for stronger relationships with the people I do still have time with. At least for now I can cherish the perspective they have given me, and for the relationships they are helping me shape. I still have grandparents who I won’t let slip me by. 

  
  I love you Ama and Kong-Kong. We may have never had the chance to get to know each other in this world. But I carry parts of you with me today, and maybe some time down the line we’ll talk about the lives we have lived. I’m sure that would make me happy.
