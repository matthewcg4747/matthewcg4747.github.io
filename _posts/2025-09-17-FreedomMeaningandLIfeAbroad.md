title: Freedom, Meaning, & Life Abroad
author: Matthew
layout: post
---
As of starting this essay, I’ve officially lived in Mongolia for one year and have decided to stay for even longer. I wanted to take this opportunity of introspection to (somewhat) address a question I’m often asked but rarely have the opportunity to address with the depth and intimacy that I think it deserves: “Why did you come to Mongolia?”

After graduating in May of 2023, I had a few short-term goals I was determined to pursue, mostly a list of familial and emotional priorities that I felt needed my attention. So instead of taking that expected “great leap” into the world, I left California and returned to Northern Virginia, the place I had called home for over 12 years. It was both a chance to accomplish what I had set out to do, and, admittedly (or more accurately, probably), an excuse to confront the lack of a confident direction and motivation that clouded my final year at college. While I made some traditional strides during my time at Pomona, there was always a quiet but persistent sense of dissatisfaction likely linked to picking a path at 16 that I had no sincere interest in walking. Eventually, this manifested into the all too familiar desire to restart my life. In the chase  for fulfillment, serenity (?) I decided that I would live abroad to make that happen, and far away at that. After closing the chapter at home, I packed my bags and moved to the mysterious and unfamiliar capital of Ulaanbaatar. I’d like to say in a sense it’s worked out, because I at least have a much better understanding of what freedom means to me.

If you were to ask most people what true freedom looks like, you’d likely encounter some version of complete and uninhibited autonomy. A life where you can be wherever you want, see whomever you want, and, most importantly, do whatever you want whenever you want. At least that’s what my algorithm would purport to suggest with feeds filled with digital nomads and travel influencers bouncing from country to country filled with sharp, novel, and exciting adventures following one after another. However, I want to ask the question, is this really freedom, or more precisely is this the kind of “freedom” we want to promote as a ubiquitous aspiration?

After living abroad for a year and distinctly choosing to stay for even longer, I’ve come to feel that freedom isn’t unlimited autonomy in many of the same ways that freedom is never going to be visual. It isn’t a fancy house, a tropical beach, or a tangible object you can point to, just as it’s not about the capacity to pick up and go wherever you want. What I’ve grown to realize is that freedom is the ability to call a place part of myself and to feel that I’ve been woven into the fabric and rhythm of a community others call home. Furthermore, I don’t view obligation and responsibility, especially to those that I care about, as the antithesis of freedom, just as I don’t see complete autonomy as its synonym. Instead, they are pieces that ground me, serving as connections to my environment -- not chains. So what exactly is the problem?

I think much of what we see online has distorted our understanding of freedom by often equating it with a place to live, aka freedom as geography. It’s a trap I myself fell into by looking for it in places like New York, Los Angeles, and eventually Ulaanbaatar. The problem with this is that people like me invariably move to cities and countries where they don’t know anybody. While we are ostensibly experiencing a freedom or “liberation” from our previous lives, we are also left without connection, inevitably leading to isolation. I believe this consequence is a significant driver behind the popularized lifestyles of digital nomads and travel influencers frequently documented online. And it ultimately underpins my largest critique of these lifestyles, in that they are essentially consumptive, operating within a dynamic predicated on asymmetry. 

Whether or not digital nomads and travel influencers admit it, the reality is that at the very least they often contribute little, if anything, to the places they visit. Wherever they end up, they tend to leverage western capital flows (including social media) to eat cheaper food, experience nature inaccessible back home, or stay in accommodations locals cannot afford. For digital nomads the experience is clear: you get to live in the proverbial ivory castle contributing some money to a cheaper economy while earning a western salary, while the traveler gets to be the audience/participant in the “show” of another country indulging in their foreign obscurity from locals who find their existence interesting and novel. So while I don’t have the time or space to develop this thread, what is really key here is that many of these people do little to nothing to leave the places they visit better than they found them. There is no meaningful contribution. For most of these people, the place was never truly there; it served to be more of a backdrop or the travel episode, not a community. As a result, they never truly belonged. 

To be clear, I’m not claiming this is a black and white issue, or that all digital nomads and travelers are pernicious agents that are harmful or exploitative. At least for this essay, I want to emphasize that freedom, as is often reported by these people online, is a freedom without belonging in addition to being inexplicably linked with western capital. And freedom without a sense of belonging isn’t real freedom, it’s merely aimless movement with the occasional veneer of liberation.

You see, when you are constantly traveling and moving around, you are essentially going to places that don’t need you and where you’re not contributing anything. Life becomes centered on spectacle or personal pleasure and convenience. Inevitably, this is a lifestyle that will, at least I would argue, for most people get very hollow. This is because you can’t obtain meaning from looking around. It can wear thin. How much can you really be learning, how much depth can you honestly reach from staying in a place for 90 days at a time, in a luxury apartment, or by constantly moving from place to place? This is what finally brings me to answering the question of why Mongolia. Which is to say, I don’t really know.

I started my journey by looking for a fresh start, which soon became a quest for freedom, and eventually morphed into a search for belonging. I chose Mongolia because it was far and it was novel, exactly the kinds of reasons I now critique in this essay. However, I learned from that choice and decided to channel it into making a conscious effort of engraing myself into the world of Ulaanbaatar and Mongolia, not keeping myself separate. And so, in the last year, while I may not have been perfect I think I’ve made progress, and in the next year I will make even more. I have felt my best when embraced with generosity and when given the chance to reciprocate, moments that have characterized the redefinition of what freedom means to me. Not in escape, but in connection. In the end, I’ve found both freedom and solace in the development of connections that serve as the platform for new stories, not just mine, to take shape and grow.

In the end, maybe I’m making a misplaced argument about semantics, allowing my American brain to fall for the influence of conflating freedom with a life of content. Regardless, I think there is one thing I want to dedicate this essay to. It’s an ode to the people that stay, because I’ve always been a runner. I’ve run from school, from life, from home, from my friends as well as relationships, from city to city, and now a whole new country. If everyone were like me, we wouldn’t have much of what makes me proud to be a part of some of the communities I champion. Because culture is built by the people who stay and contribute. No matter how boring, overlooked, difficult, or unassuming a place may be perceived, it’s the people who invest in its texture, memory, and meaning that make it a home worth living. 

I’m incredibly blessed to have been able to live a life abroad, especially in a city so full of compassion and dimension. And it is precisely because of the people who stayed. This is not a privilege that I take lightly, and in my own little way, I hope that in choosing to stay longer I can contribute something of value to the people who have already shared with me so much. Even if it is just a single grain of sand in the vast desert of the Gobi, I hope my story can be part of something bigger, something shared and rooted in the country and people who have welcomed me with open arms.
